# Applied Quiz: Invasive Species Removal

A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward using the ideas
rather than recalling them. Each one puts you in a situation and asks what the
chapter's framework says to do.

---

#### 1. A volunteer group spends a Saturday cutting every buckthorn stem on a woodland lot. They do not treat the stumps. What does this chapter predict for next season?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>The buckthorn will be gone</li>
<li>The site will be worse — each cut stump can send up five to ten new stems, so the stand comes back denser than before</li>
<li>The buckthorn will return at roughly the same density</li>
<li>Native plants will fill the gaps before buckthorn regrows</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Bree calls this "the buckthorn trap," and the chapter names cutting without follow-up treatment as one of the most common and costly mistakes in invasive management. A day of hard work can leave the site harder to fix than before it started.

    **Concept Tested:** Buckthorn Resprouting

#### 2. A contractor offers to clear your buckthorn "completely, in one season, guaranteed." Using this chapter, how should you read that?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Accept — professional equipment can finish the job in one pass</li>
<li>Treat it skeptically; buckthorn seeds stay viable in the soil for five years or more, so the chapter says anyone promising a one-season fix is mistaken or selling something</li>
<li>Accept, but ask for a two-year warranty</li>
<li>Refuse all professional help and do it by hand</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter states this almost word for word. The adult plants are the visible half of the problem; hundreds of seeds per square meter in the soil are the other half, and no single-season treatment reaches them.

    **Concept Tested:** Buckthorn Seed Bank

#### 3. After clearing buckthorn, a landowner rototills the whole site to prepare for native seeding. What does this chapter say is wrong with that?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Nothing — tilling is standard site preparation</li>
<li>Disturbed soil exposes buried buckthorn seed to light and stimulates germination, so tilling can trigger a flush of exactly what was just removed</li>
<li>Tilling kills native seed only</li>
<li>Tilling is fine as long as herbicide was used first</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. "Minimize soil disturbance" is one of the chapter's seed-bank rules, sitting right alongside establishing dense native cover to compete with seedlings. Removal already increases light at ground level; adding soil disturbance compounds it.

    **Concept Tested:** Managing the Seed Bank

    **See:** [Chapter 9](index.md)

#### 4. You pull garlic mustard in late May and pile the plants at the edge of the woods to compost. What has gone wrong?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Nothing — pulled plants are dead</li>
<li>Garlic mustard can keep ripening seed after it is pulled, so the pile becomes a seed source; the chapter says to bag pulled plants and remove them from the site</li>
<li>May is too early to pull</li>
<li>Composting is fine but the plants should be chopped first</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. This is a specific, easily-made error with a specific fix. The plant has enough stored resources to finish maturing seed pods after being uprooted, which is why "bag and remove" is a separate instruction from "pull."

    **Concept Tested:** Garlic Mustard Removal

#### 5. A land manager has budget to work on either a high-quality remnant prairie with a small new knapweed patch, or a badly degraded lot completely overrun with it. What does the chapter's prioritization say?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>The degraded lot, because it has more invasive plants</li>
<li>The remnant — protect your best remaining native areas first, and small early infestations are far cheaper to control than established ones; both criteria point the same way</li>
<li>Split the budget evenly</li>
<li>Neither; wait for more funding</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Two of the chapter's four prioritization criteria — ecological value of the site and stage of invasion — line up here. Spending the same money on the overrun lot buys much less ecological return and probably does not finish the job.

    **Concept Tested:** Prioritizing Removal Efforts

#### 6. You cut a large buckthorn stem at 9 a.m. and plan to come back with herbicide after lunch. What does this chapter say about that plan?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Fine — timing within the same day makes no difference</li>
<li>Treat the stump immediately; even a few hours of delay reduces effectiveness as the cut surface begins to seal</li>
<li>Wait a full day so the stump dries</li>
<li>Herbicide should be applied before cutting</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter's timing principles make this explicit. The open cut surface is the delivery route to the root system, and the plant starts closing it right away.

    **Concept Tested:** Cut-Stump Treatment Timing

#### 7. Why does the chapter recommend late summer and fall for woody invasive removal rather than spring?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>The weather is more comfortable for volunteers</li>
<li>In late summer and fall plants are moving carbohydrates down to their roots, so herbicide applied to a cut stump travels with that flow and reaches the root system</li>
<li>Herbicide is cheaper in fall</li>
<li>Woody plants are dormant and cannot resprout</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The reasoning is about the direction of flow inside the plant. Treating with the downward carbohydrate movement carries the herbicide where it needs to go; treating against it in spring is far less effective.

    **Concept Tested:** Seasonal Timing of Herbicide Treatment

    **See:** [Chapter 9](index.md)

#### 8. A group pulls garlic mustard from the same woodland every spring for four years, then skips year five because the population looks small. What does this chapter say happens?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Nothing — four years is enough to exhaust the seed bank</li>
<li>Skipping even a single year can undo several years of progress, because the surviving plants set seed and reload the seed bank</li>
<li>The population will decline on its own by then</li>
<li>Native plants will outcompete it after four years</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter reports this from land managers directly, and puts the required commitment at four to six years. A biennial that produces hundreds of seeds per plant only needs one unattended season to reset the clock.

    **Concept Tested:** Persistence in Garlic Mustard Control

    **See:** [Chapter 9](index.md)

#### 9. After removing invasives from a woodland, what does this chapter say monitoring should watch for besides regrowth of the original species?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Only regrowth matters</li>
<li>New seedlings from the seed bank, <em>different</em> invasive species colonizing the disturbed area, and whether natives are actually returning or the ground is still bare</li>
<li>Only whether natives return</li>
<li>Soil pH changes</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Removal creates a disturbed, well-lit opening, which is precisely the condition a different invader can exploit. The chapter's monitoring list covers all four items, and the site can fail in any of them.

    **Concept Tested:** Follow-Up Monitoring

#### 10. A neighbor mows a patch of Wild Parsnip once in early August, after it has flowered. Evaluate that decision.

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Well timed — August mowing kills the plant outright</li>
<li>Too late; the chapter's most important timing rule is to remove before seed set, and cutting a flowered plant in August risks having already added to the seed bank — the recommended approach is cutting the root below the crown in spring, wearing protection</li>
<li>Correct, but he should have used herbicide</li>
<li>Timing does not matter for biennials</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. "Remove before seed set" is the chapter's single most important timing rule, because a badly timed effort adds to the problem rather than reducing it. The species-specific guidance for wild parsnip is a spring root cut below the crown, with protective clothing against the sap.

    **Concept Tested:** Removal Timing and Seed Set
