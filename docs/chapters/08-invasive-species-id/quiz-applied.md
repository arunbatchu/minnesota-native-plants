# Applied Quiz: Invasive Species Identification

A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward using the ideas
rather than recalling them. Each one puts you in a situation and asks what the
chapter's framework says to do.

---

#### 1. You are unsure whether a shrub is Common Buckthorn. What single test does this chapter give you that needs nothing but a pocket knife?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Count the berries per cluster</li>
<li>Scrape the bark — Common Buckthorn's inner bark is a distinctive yellow-orange</li>
<li>Measure the height</li>
<li>Check whether it has thorns, which only buckthorn has</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The yellow-orange inner bark is diagnostic and works year-round, unlike leaves or berries. Option D is a trap: buckthorn does carry small twig-tip thorns, but plenty of native shrubs have thorns too, so their presence alone confirms nothing.

    **Concept Tested:** Identifying Common Buckthorn

#### 2. A November walk through your woods shows one shrub species still fully green while everything else is bare. What does this chapter say you are looking at, and why does it matter beyond identification?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>A healthy native shrub with a long season</li>
<li>Very likely buckthorn — late leaf retention is both a field mark and a competitive weapon, because the extended season lets it photosynthesize when natives cannot and shade the ground when spring plants need light</li>
<li>A plant stressed by drought</li>
<li>An evergreen native</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter lists early leaf-out and late leaf retention among the traits that make invasives succeed, and separately gives fall green-up as the seasonal timing clue for buckthorn. The same trait is the clue and the cause of harm.

    **Concept Tested:** Why Invasive Species Succeed

    **See:** [Chapter 8](index.md)

#### 3. A fisherman dumps leftover bait worms at the edge of a northern Minnesota forest. Why is this a serious problem rather than a harmless act?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>It isn't — earthworms improve any soil</li>
<li>Minnesota's northern forests developed without earthworms after glaciation, and European species consume the duff layer that native wildflowers need to germinate; there is no practical way to remove them once established, and dumping bait is illegal in Minnesota</li>
<li>The worms will die in the cold and have no effect</li>
<li>It only affects fishing, not forests</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. This is the chapter's most counterintuitive entry. Earthworms are beneficial in a garden and destructive in a forest that evolved without them, and the irreversibility is what makes prevention the only real control. Bree's warning notes it is also the law.

    **Concept Tested:** European Earthworms

    **See:** [Chapter 8](index.md)

#### 4. Hiking in northern Minnesota you notice bare mineral soil where you expected thick leaf litter, few wildflowers, and a lot of Pennsylvania Sedge. What should you suspect?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Recent logging</li>
<li>Earthworm invasion — a missing duff layer, simplified understory, loss of Trillium and Wild Ginger, and sedge dominance are the standard signs</li>
<li>Drought</li>
<li>Deer overbrowsing only</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter is explicit that you diagnose earthworms by their effects rather than by finding the animals. All four signs listed in the question are on its list, and together they are a much stronger signal than any one alone.

    **Concept Tested:** Signs of Earthworm Invasion

    **See:** [Chapter 8](index.md)

#### 5. You find what you believe is a new infestation of an invasive species in a county park. What does this chapter say is the highest-value action?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Pull it yourself immediately</li>
<li>Document it — photos, GPS or a detailed location, estimated area, habitat, date — and report through EDDMapS or the MN DNR, because early reports can trigger rapid response before the population becomes unmanageable</li>
<li>Post about it on social media</li>
<li>Nothing; land managers already know</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter's reporting flowchart ends in "rapid response if early detection," and that pathway only exists if someone reports. Option A is well-meant but skips the step that lets managers act at landscape scale, and on public land it may not be permitted.

    **Concept Tested:** Reporting Invasive Species

    **See:** [Chapter 8](index.md)

#### 6. Garlic Mustard is described as changing the soil itself. What is the mechanism, and why does it matter for the trees?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>It acidifies the soil by dropping leaves</li>
<li>It is allelopathic — it releases chemicals that suppress the soil fungi many native trees depend on, so the damage outlasts the plant</li>
<li>It physically compacts the soil</li>
<li>It fixes nitrogen, over-enriching the site</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Allelopathy appears in the chapter's list of traits that make invasives succeed, and garlic mustard is its clearest local case. Because it attacks the mycorrhizal partnerships tree seedlings need, clearing the plant does not immediately restore the site.

    **Concept Tested:** Allelopathy

    **See:** [Chapter 8](index.md)

#### 7. You are planning a volunteer workday to remove Wild Parsnip on a July afternoon. What does the chapter require you to plan for?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Nothing special — it pulls like any weed</li>
<li>Full skin protection: long sleeves, gloves and eye protection, because the sap contains furanocoumarins that cause severe burns and blistering when exposed skin meets sunlight</li>
<li>Only gloves, since the sap is a mild irritant</li>
<li>Removal is illegal without a permit</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Phytophotodermatitis needs two ingredients — sap and sunlight — which is exactly what a summer afternoon workday provides. If sap does contact skin, the chapter says wash immediately and keep that area out of the sun.

    **Concept Tested:** Wild Parsnip Health Hazard

    **See:** [Chapter 8](index.md)

#### 8. Creeping Charlie is on the DNR's invasive list but not the Noxious Weed List. What practical difference does that make?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>None — the lists mean the same thing</li>
<li>There is no legal requirement to control it, because it mainly takes over lawns and disturbed ground rather than threatening healthy natural areas; treat it as a persistent yard weed, not a woodland emergency</li>
<li>It means the plant is harmless</li>
<li>It means it is more dangerous than buckthorn</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter draws this distinction to help readers triage. "Invasive" and "regulated noxious weed" are different categories with different obligations, and knowing which is which tells you where to spend limited effort.

    **Concept Tested:** Invasive vs. Regulated Noxious Weed

#### 9. Why does this chapter say invasive species do so well specifically on roadsides, construction sites and degraded lots?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Those places have the richest soil</li>
<li>Tolerance of disturbed soils is one of the traits invasives share; natives adapted to stable communities establish poorly there, so disturbance hands invaders an open field</li>
<li>Road salt kills native plants only</li>
<li>Seeds are deliberately planted there for erosion control</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Disturbance tolerance is on the chapter's list of advantages. This is why linear corridors act as invasion highways across the state, and why a construction project can seed a problem that spreads well beyond its own footprint.

    **Concept Tested:** Disturbance and Invasion

    **See:** [Chapter 8](index.md)

#### 10. A wetland is full of tall grass forming tan monotypic stands in winter. Someone proposes mowing it for hay. What does the chapter suggest is growing there and why does the plan carry risk?

<ol type="A" class="upper-alpha">
<li>Native Blue Joint Grass; haying it is beneficial</li>
<li>Reed Canary Grass, which forms dense monocultures that exclude other wetland plants — and mowing equipment moving between sites is a route for spreading it further</li>
<li>Wild Rice, which is protected</li>
<li>Switchgrass, a desirable native</li>
</ol>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Tan monotypic winter stands in a Minnesota wetland are the chapter's own seasonal clue for Reed Canary Grass. The monoculture is the identification and the harm at once, and equipment is a well-known vector between sites.

    **Concept Tested:** Reed Canary Grass
