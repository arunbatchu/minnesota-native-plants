    3. 16-Critical Thinking and Misinfo

    Applied Quiz: Critical Thinking and Misinformation

    A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward using the ideas rather than recalling them. Each one puts you in a situation and asks what the chapter's framework says to do.

    1. A neighbor says his family has gardened here for 30 years and never had a buckthorn problem, so the warnings are overblown. What is the reasoning error?

    1. He is right — direct long-term experience outweighs studies
    2. Anecdote is not data; his account tells you about his yard and his definition of "problem," and does not contradict extensive research documenting buckthorn's damage statewide
    3. He is wrong because he is not a trained botanist
    4. Thirty years is too short a period to judge
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter uses almost this exact example. Note that option C is itself a fallacy the chapter names — ad hominem attacks the person rather than the claim, and it is the wrong way to be right.

    Concept Tested: Anecdotal Evidence vs. Data

    See: Chapter 16

    2. A store sells a "Pollinator Garden Kit" in green packaging with a bee logo, no species list and no seed source. How does this chapter suggest you evaluate it?

    1. Buy it — the pollinator label indicates ecological benefit
    2. Treat it as likely greenwashing; the tell is vagueness, and the antidote is specificity — a legitimate product lists exactly which native species are included and where the seed was sourced
    3. The packaging color is irrelevant to product quality
    4. Assume it is fine if sold by a major retailer
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter gives this as a real example, noting such kits often contain mostly non-native annuals and may carry neonicotinoid-treated seed. Claims that cannot be checked because no specifics are provided is one of its listed greenwashing signals.

    Concept Tested: Greenwashing Detection

    See: Chapter 16

    3. Someone argues: "Either we spray herbicide on every invasive or we give up and do nothing." Name the fallacy and the correction.

    1. Appeal to authority; defer to a professional applicator
    2. False dilemma — there are many approaches including mechanical removal, targeted application, biological control, and combinations of methods
    3. Slippery slope; the argument predicts an inevitable outcome
    4. Cherry-picking; the speaker selected favorable evidence
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A false dilemma presents two options as exhaustive when they are not, and this is the chapter's own illustration of it. Chapter 9's integrated pest management section is the substantive answer to why the middle ground exists.

    Concept Tested: Logical Fallacies

    See: Chapter 16

    4. A gardener who dislikes the idea of removing her buckthorn finds one blog post saying it is "not that bad" and cites it repeatedly. What is happening?

    1. She is doing responsible independent research
    2. Confirmation bias — seeking and remembering information that confirms an existing belief while dismissing contradicting evidence; the counter is to ask what evidence would change her mind
    3. She is applying the precautionary principle
    4. She is correctly weighting a Tier 3 source
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter uses this exact scenario. Its counters are practical: actively seek information that challenges your view, weigh strength of evidence rather than agreement, and be most suspicious of claims that feel satisfying or vindicating.

    Concept Tested: Confirmation Bias

    See: Chapter 16

    5. You read that buckthorn berries feed birds, so buckthorn has some ecological value. How does this chapter address that?

    1. It agrees — any wildlife food source has net value
    2. Birds do eat the berries, but they have a laxative effect that reduces the birds' ability to build migration fat, and buckthorn's dense shade eliminates the native plants providing higher-quality food, nesting habitat and insect prey — a food source that degrades the ecosystem is not a net benefit
    3. Birds do not actually eat buckthorn berries
    4. The claim is irrelevant since birds are not the concern
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter treats this as a myth built on a true observation, which is what makes it durable. The right move is not denying the observation but following it through to the full accounting.

    Concept Tested: Invasive Species Myths

    See: Chapter 16

    6. A viral post reads: "This Minnesota wildflower is going EXTINCT — share before it's too late!" with no source. Which patterns does this chapter flag?

    1. None — raising awareness is always beneficial
    2. Manufactured urgency and extreme language with no cited source; social media algorithms reward engagement rather than accuracy, so emotionally provocative content spreads faster than careful information
    3. Only that the species name is missing
    4. Appeal to tradition
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter lists manufactured urgency among its social media misinformation patterns, and advises skepticism toward extreme framing. Its constructive suggestion is to follow extension services, botanical gardens and established conservation organizations rather than anonymous viral pages.

    Concept Tested: Social Media Misinformation

    See: Chapter 16

    7. Your clay soil drains poorly and a neighbor suggests working in a few yards of sand. What does this chapter say?

    1. Good advice — sand is the standard fix for clay
    2. A myth worth resisting: sand added to clay can produce a concrete-like mixture. Add organic matter instead, or choose the many native prairie species that do well in heavy clay
    3. Sand works but only in large quantities
    4. Clay soil cannot support native plants
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter lists this among common plant myths, and it connects to a theme running through the whole book — match the plant to the site rather than re-engineering the site. Wild Bergamot, Joe-Pye Weed and New England Aster all handle clay.

    Concept Tested: Common Plant Myths

    See: Chapter 16

    8. You need to evaluate a claim about neonicotinoids and bees. Rank these sources using the chapter's tier framework: a University of Minnesota Extension page, a nursery's product page, and a peer-reviewed journal article.

    1. Nursery page, Extension, journal article
    2. Journal article and Extension are both Tier 1; the nursery product page is Tier 3 marketing material to use with caution
    3. All three carry equal weight
    4. Only the journal article has any value
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Peer-reviewed journals, university extension services and government scientific agencies share Tier 1. The chapter is careful that lower tiers are not worthless — an experienced gardener's blog can be valuable — but you check whether it cites sources and aligns with higher tiers.

    Concept Tested: Source Credibility Tiers

    See: Chapter 16

    9. Minnesota bans the sale of certain invasive species even where the ecological evidence is still developing. Which principle does this chapter say is at work?

    1. Appeal to authority
    2. The precautionary principle — where potential harm is severe and irreversible but evidence is uncertain, err toward caution, because preventing an invasion costs far less than managing an established one
    3. Cost-benefit analysis showing certain net benefit
    4. Confirmation bias in policymaking
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The asymmetry between prevention cost and management cost is what justifies acting before the evidence is complete. This is also why the Noxious Weed Law's "Prohibited Eradicate" tier exists for species not yet widespread.

    Concept Tested: The Precautionary Principle

    See: Chapter 16

    10. A designer insists straight species are "always the right choice" over cultivars. How does this chapter's approach to trade-offs sharpen that?

    1. She is right — there is no legitimate case for cultivars
    2. Making the trade-off explicit is better than declaring one option universally best: straight species generally carry higher ecological value, while cultivars offer more predictable size, color and bloom for designed landscapes — the trade is ecological function against design control
    3. Cultivars are always better for gardens
    4. The choice makes no measurable difference
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter calls making trade-offs explicit a hallmark of good critical thinking. That does not make the options equal — Chapter 11 still says use wild type for restoration — but it names what you give up either way instead of pretending there is no cost.
