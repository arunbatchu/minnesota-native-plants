    Applied Quiz: Plant Identification Skills

    A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward using the ideas rather than recalling them. Each one puts you in a situation and asks what the chapter's framework says to do.

    1. You find a yellow umbel-flowered plant on a roadside in early July. It stands about 4 feet tall with grooved, angular stems. What should you conclude, and what should you not do?

    1. Golden Alexanders — a native worth protecting; pull the weeds around it
    2. Wild Parsnip — the later bloom, greater height and grooved angular stems all point away from Golden Alexanders; do not handle it bare-skinned, because the sap causes severe burns in sunlight
    3. Either one; the two cannot be distinguished in the field
    4. Wild Parsnip — pull it immediately with bare hands before it seeds
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Three features agree: Golden Alexanders blooms May–June with smooth stems and stays shorter, while Wild Parsnip blooms June–July, reaches 5 feet, and has grooved angular stems. Option D gets the identification right and the safety badly wrong — phytophotodermatitis from parsnip sap causes blistering burns.

    2. A plant ID app returns "Kentucky Bluegrass, 61% confidence" for a grass you photographed in a remnant prairie. How much should you trust it?

    1. Fully — apps are more accurate than human observers
    2. Not much; the chapter names grasses, sedges and rushes as exactly where apps are weakest, and a moderate confidence score on a hard group is a prompt to check stem shape, ligule and inflorescence yourself
    3. Not at all — apps are never useful
    4. Fully, because 61% is a majority
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter lists four situations where apps struggle, and grasses top the list because the models find them as confusable as people do. The right response is to fall back on your own observation, not to abandon the tool or accept it uncritically.

    Concept Tested: Limits of Plant ID Apps

    See: Chapter 7

    3. You are 70% sure a shrub in your woods is buckthorn but not certain. What does this chapter tell you to do?

    1. Pull it — the risk of leaving an invasive outweighs everything
    2. Leave it and come back when you can confirm; removing a native by mistake does more harm than leaving an invasive for another day
    3. Spray it with herbicide as a precaution
    4. Ask a neighbor to decide
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. "When in doubt, don't pull" is one of the chapter's stated general rules. The asymmetry is the reasoning: buckthorn will still be there next week, and a native you destroy is gone. Option C is worse than A, since herbicide damage is not reversible either.

    Concept Tested: Identification Confidence and Removal Decisions

    See: Chapter 7

    4. Walking in January, you want to identify trees. A friend says to come back in June. Using this chapter, what would you tell her?

    1. She's right — trees cannot be identified without leaves
    2. Winter is a legitimate ID season and some botanists prefer it: bark, buds, branching silhouette and persistent seed heads are all visible without leaves in the way
    3. Only conifers can be identified in winter
    4. Winter ID works but only for shrubs
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Bree's key insight in this chapter makes the point that leaves can actually obscure the features that distinguish trees — an American Elm's vase silhouette and a Bur Oak's gnarled branching read more clearly bare. Each season offers a different set of clues rather than a better or worse one.

    See: Chapter 7

    5. You encounter a non-woody plant with narrow parallel-veined leaves. You roll the stem and it feels distinctly triangular. Where does the workflow put you?

    1. Grass — check the ligule next
    2. Sedge — triangular solid stems separate sedges from grasses (round, hollow, with nodes) and rushes (round, solid, no nodes)
    3. Forb — parallel veins indicate a wildflower
    4. Rush — triangular stems are the rush signature
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The workflow's stem-shape branch does the work: triangular and solid means sedge. This is "sedges have edges" turned into a decision point, and it is the fastest way past the group most people find intimidating.

    Concept Tested: Field Identification Workflow

    See: Chapter 7

    6. You find a woody shrub with opposite leaves in a Minnesota woodland. How does MADCap Horse narrow the field?

    1. It doesn't — opposite leaves are too common to be useful
    2. Most woody plants have alternate leaves, so opposite leaves point you toward maple, ash, dogwood, the honeysuckle family, or horse chestnut — a short list from a large one
    3. It tells you the plant is invasive
    4. It identifies the exact species
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The mnemonic works because opposite arrangement is the uncommon case among woody plants, so noticing it eliminates most candidates in one observation. It narrows rather than names, which is why option D overstates it.

    7. Walking a woodland edge, you notice a dense stand of one species with nothing else growing under it. What should that pattern make you check?

    1. Nothing — dense single-species stands are normal in healthy woods
    2. Whether it is invasive; the chapter names monocultures as a red flag, since invasives commonly form dense single-species stands that crowd everything else out
    3. Whether the soil is too fertile
    4. Whether deer have been browsing
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Context is one of the chapter's identification tools, not just a background detail. A native plant community is normally mixed; uniformity over a large patch is a structural clue that something is outcompeting its neighbors.

    Concept Tested: Using Context in Identification

    See: Chapter 7

    8. You spot Monarch caterpillars feeding on an unfamiliar plant. What can you conclude and why?

    1. Nothing — caterpillars eat many plants
    2. It is a milkweed; monarch larvae feed only on Asclepias, so the specialist insect confirms the plant's identity
    3. The plant is invasive, since monarchs prefer non-natives
    4. The plant is a nectar source but not necessarily a milkweed
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter lists insects among its summer identification clues for exactly this reason. Specialist relationships run both ways as evidence — a strict host relationship means the insect is a reliable indicator of the plant.

    Concept Tested: Insects as Identification Clues

    See: Chapter 7

    9. Someone identifies a vine as native Wild Grape based on the fruit alone, which is dark purple. What does the chapter's general rule say about that?

    1. Fruit color is definitive for vines
    2. Never rely on a single characteristic — compare leaf shape, stem structure, bloom time and habitat together; Porcelain Berry's fruit can also darken, and its deeply lobed leaves are the better separator
    3. The identification is wrong; wild grape fruit is green
    4. Vines cannot be identified without flowers
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. "Check multiple features" is the chapter's first general rule for separating natives from invasive look-alikes. Wild Grape has simple lobed leaves; Porcelain Berry's are deeply lobed, and that structural difference is more reliable than a color that varies with ripeness.

    Concept Tested: Checking Multiple Features

    See: Chapter 7

    10. After identifying a plant you are unsure about, what does this chapter recommend as the final step, and what does it get you?

    1. Nothing further — once you have a name you are done
    2. Record the observation with photos and notes and upload it to iNaturalist, which gets you community verification and contributes the record to a public dataset
    3. Collect a pressed specimen for a herbarium
    4. Remove the plant if it turns out to be non-native
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Recording is step six of the workflow. It closes the loop for you personally — other observers correct or confirm the call — and the observation becomes data other people can use, which is a real contribution from an amateur identification.
