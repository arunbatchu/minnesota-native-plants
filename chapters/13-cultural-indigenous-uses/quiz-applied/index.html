    3. 13-Cultural and Indigenous Uses

    Applied Quiz: Cultural and Indigenous Uses

    A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward using the ideas rather than recalling them. Each one puts you in a situation and asks what the chapter's framework says to do.

    1. A guidebook describes Minnesota's oak savannas as "pristine wilderness untouched by human hands before 1850." What does this chapter say is wrong with that?

    1. Nothing — pre-contact Minnesota was genuinely unmanaged
    2. It is historically inaccurate; the prairies, savannas and open woodlands settlers described were cultural landscapes shaped by generations of Indigenous management, especially fire
    3. It is right about savannas but wrong about prairies
    4. The landscapes were shaped by climate alone
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter calls this "the myth of wilderness" and draws a direct consequence for restoration: the goal is not returning to a pre-human state but reviving a human-plant partnership that sustained both the land and the people.

    Concept Tested: The Myth of Wilderness

    See: Chapter 13

    2. A wellness company markets a "traditional Native American herbal blend," citing Ojibwe practice, without involving any Ojibwe community. What does this chapter call this, and what would ethical practice look like?

    1. Fair use of public knowledge
    2. Appropriation, and potentially biopiracy — ethical practice requires free, prior and informed consent, community ownership of data, and benefit-sharing when the work leads to commercial gain
    3. Acceptable as long as the company credits "Native American tradition" generally
    4. A legal question only, with no ethical dimension
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter names biopiracy specifically as using Indigenous knowledge for commercial products without compensating the originating community. Option C fails the chapter's own instruction to credit Indigenous knowledge rather than presenting it as generic folk wisdom.

    Concept Tested: Ethics of Ethnobotany and Appropriation

    See: Chapter 13

    3. Why does this chapter say Indigenous plant knowledge is not simply a list of plant uses?

    1. Because the uses have been forgotten
    2. Because it is relational, place-based, holistic and dynamic — gathering is understood as a relationship involving permission, thanks and restraint, not a transaction with a resource
    3. Because the knowledge was never written down
    4. Because most of the plants are now extinct
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter lists all four characteristics. Bree's admonition frames the contrast: Western science asks what compounds a plant contains, while Indigenous knowledge asks what relationship and responsibilities come with it — and neither approach is complete without the other.

    Concept Tested: Characteristics of Indigenous Plant Knowledge

    See: Chapter 13

    4. You want to forage ramps in a Minnesota state park. What does this chapter tell you to check first?

    1. Nothing — public land is open for harvest
    2. The regulations: many parks and preserves restrict or prohibit harvesting, and you also need to consider whether the population can sustain removal and whose homeland the land is
    3. Only whether you can identify the plant correctly
    4. Whether other foragers have been there first
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's harvesting principles stack several checks: legal protections, sustainability of the population, the one-tenth guideline, and awareness that Minnesota is Dakota and Ojibwe homeland. Correct identification matters too, but it is not the first question.

    Concept Tested: Wild Harvest Ethics

    See: Chapter 13

    5. A forager wants a reliable annual supply of Wild Bergamot for tea. What does the chapter recommend?

    1. Harvest a different wild population each year to spread the impact
    2. Grow it — many native plant nurseries sell traditionally used species, and growing your own removes the pressure from wild populations entirely
    3. Harvest up to half of each population, since bergamot is common
    4. Buy it from any online herbal supplier
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Bree's tip makes this the practical resolution of the whole harvest-ethics section. Cultivation converts an extractive habit into a sustainable one, and it sidesteps the identification and legal questions at the same time.

    Concept Tested: Sustainable Alternatives to Wild Harvest

    See: Chapter 13

    6. Big Bluestem regrows vigorously after a burn while nearby shrubs are killed back. What does this chapter say that reveals about the plant's history?

    1. It is a coincidence of plant anatomy
    2. These communities co-evolved with Indigenous burning over thousands of years — the growing points sit below the soil surface because fire was a regular, human-maintained part of the environment
    3. The grasses adapted to natural lightning fires only
    4. The shrubs are non-native and therefore fire-sensitive
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter is explicit that this is not a coincidence. Fire was predictable because people made it predictable, and the plant community's adaptations are a record of that relationship.

    Concept Tested: Fire and Plant Adaptation

    See: Chapter 13

    7. A land manager describes prescribed burning as "a modern scientific technique." Using this chapter, how would you complicate that?

    1. It is accurate — prescribed fire was invented by twentieth-century ecologists
    2. Modern practice draws directly on Indigenous burning knowledge, a debt the chapter notes is not always acknowledged; several tribal nations are now leading cultural burning initiatives
    3. Prescribed burning has no relationship to Indigenous practice
    4. Indigenous burning was accidental rather than managed
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter says the debt often goes unacknowledged, and describes a movement to return fire management authority to Indigenous communities — restoring the cultural dimension of the practice alongside the ecological one.

    Concept Tested: Reviving Cultural Burning

    See: Chapter 13

    8. A reader wants to learn to use native plants medicinally from what this chapter provides. What does the chapter itself say about that?

    1. The chapter is a sufficient guide to safe herbal practice
    2. It does not provide medical advice; traditional medicine operated within systems of extensive training, spiritual practice and community support, and self-treating from incomplete information can be dangerous since plants safe when properly prepared can be toxic when misused
    3. Any plant listed in the chapter is safe to consume
    4. Medicinal knowledge is fully public and freely available
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's caution is direct. Option D also fails a point the chapter makes about intellectual sovereignty: identity and preparation of medicinal plants are often sacred knowledge and not meant for public sharing.

    Concept Tested: Cautions on Medicinal Plant Use

    See: Chapter 13

    9. Why does this chapter share only some Indigenous plant knowledge rather than everything researchers have documented?

    1. The rest has been lost
    2. Intellectual sovereignty — the knowledge belongs to the communities that hold it, some is sacred or restricted, and the chapter shares only what has been published with community consent or is general public knowledge
    3. The remaining knowledge is unreliable
    4. Space limitations in the textbook
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. This is a deliberate editorial position, not an omission. The chapter's recommendation follows from it: seek out Indigenous-led educational programs and publications rather than extracting knowledge from communities.

    Concept Tested: Intellectual Sovereignty

    See: Chapter 13

    10. Besides fire, what other land-management methods does this chapter credit to Indigenous stewardship in Minnesota?

    1. Fire was the only method used
    2. Selective harvesting to maintain diversity, transplanting and tending useful plants, maintaining trails and gathering sites, and modifying hydrology to support wild rice beds
    3. Plowing and irrigation
    4. Fencing and livestock grazing
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter lists five methods with fire as the most significant among them. Hydrology management for manoomin is a particularly clear case of deliberate landscape engineering in service of a food plant.

    Concept Tested: Traditional Land Management

    See: Chapter 13
