    Applied Quiz: Minnesota Resources

    A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward using the ideas rather than recalling them. Each one puts you in a situation and asks what the chapter's framework says to do.

    1. You get a city code violation notice for "weeds over 10 inches" on your two-year-old prairie planting. What does this chapter suggest you do first?

    1. Mow it immediately and give up on the planting
    2. Check whether your city offers a managed native landscape permit or registration — a growing number of Minnesota cities do, and they protect native gardens from weed complaints if standards like maintained edges, signage and setbacks are met
    3. Ignore the notice; native plants are exempt statewide
    4. Sue the city for discrimination against native plants
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter explains that height ordinances were written with mowed lawns in mind and can collide with native plantings, and that many cities have adopted exemptions or permits. Bree's advice is to check before you plant — a call to city hall is cheaper than a violation.

    Concept Tested: Local Ordinances and Native Landscape Permits

    See: Chapter 14

    2. A neighbor reports your Wild Bergamot and Little Bluestem to the city as "noxious weeds." Using this chapter, what is the substantive response?

    1. He is right — any tall vegetation is a noxious weed
    2. Minnesota's Noxious Weed Law designates specific species in defined categories; native prairie plants are not on those lists, and knowing the law is what distinguishes genuinely harmful weeds from natives mistakenly reported as weeds
    3. Noxious weed status is decided by each neighbor's judgment
    4. Native plants are automatically noxious if over 12 inches tall
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter names this dynamic directly: some municipalities have used weed ordinances against tall native plantings, and understanding the statute's actual categories is the defense. The law lists species, not heights.

    Concept Tested: MN Noxious Weed Law

    See: Chapter 14

    3. A homeowner wants to convert part of her lawn to pollinator habitat but cannot afford the plants. Which program does this chapter identify as the most accessible for an individual?

    1. The Lessard-Sams Outdoor Heritage Council, which funds habitat restoration
    2. Lawns to Legumes — a state program providing coaching and cost-share grants directly to homeowners converting lawn to pollinator plantings
    3. The Environment and Natural Resources Trust Fund
    4. There is no funding available to individuals
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter calls Lawns to Legumes one of the most accessible programs for individual homeowners. LSOHC and ENRTF are real and well funded, but they operate at project and research scale rather than paying for a residential bed. Local SWCD cost-share is the other homeowner-scale option.

    Concept Tested: State Grant Programs

    See: Chapter 14

    4. You find a state-listed threatened plant species on land you own and want to move it out of the way of a construction project. What does this chapter say?

    1. Owners may do as they like with plants on their own land
    2. Minnesota maintains its own list of endangered, threatened and special concern plants, and it is illegal to harvest, transplant or destroy them without a permit
    3. Only federally listed species are protected
    4. Transplanting is always permitted; only destruction is prohibited
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter is explicit that the state list is separate from the federal one and carries its own protections, and that transplanting is among the restricted actions. Option C is a common and consequential misunderstanding.

    Concept Tested: MN Endangered and Threatened Species Protections

    See: Chapter 14

    5. Your HOA covenant bans "unmowed vegetation." Can the city simply override it?

    1. Yes — city ordinances always supersede HOA covenants
    2. No; cities cannot override private HOA covenants, though some Minnesota cities have adopted "right to garden" policies that limit an HOA's ability to ban native plantings outright
    3. Yes, but only if you obtain a native landscape permit
    4. HOA covenants have no legal force in Minnesota
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter draws this line carefully. A city permit resolves your relationship with the city, not with a private covenant, which is why the state-level right-to-garden legislation it mentions matters separately.

    Concept Tested: HOA Restrictions and Right-to-Garden Policy

    See: Chapter 14

    6. Someone dismisses roadside vegetation as ecologically trivial. Using this chapter, why is MnDOT's management significant?

    1. It isn't — roadsides are too narrow to matter
    2. MnDOT manages over 800,000 acres of roadside and has adopted pollinator-friendly mowing schedules and native seeding, which makes it one of the largest habitat management programs in the state
    3. Roadsides are only useful for erosion control
    4. Roadside plantings are all non-native
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Scale is the argument. Individually narrow, roadsides sum to a very large connected acreage, and state law also requires MnDOT and county highway departments to consider native species in those plantings.

    Concept Tested: Roadside Vegetation Management

    See: Chapter 14

    7. A gardener wants free, local, expert advice on a native planting problem. Which resource does this chapter point to?

    1. Only a paid landscape consultant
    2. County-level resources — the Soil and Water Conservation District and the county Master Gardener program through University of Minnesota Extension
    3. The Department of Natural Resources enforcement line
    4. The Minnesota Landscape Arboretum's admissions desk
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter's county-level section exists because this is where an individual homeowner actually gets help — SWCDs administer cost-share and technical assistance, and Master Gardeners provide research-based advice through Extension.

    Concept Tested: County-Level Programs

    See: Chapter 14

    8. A seed vendor sells a "prairie mix" with no species list, purity figures or germination rates on the label. What does this chapter tell you?

    1. Seed labeling is unregulated in Minnesota
    2. The State Seed Law regulates sale and labeling of seed including native mixes, and vendors must meet requirements for species composition, purity and germination — a mix without that information is a problem
    3. Labeling rules apply only to agricultural crop seed
    4. Only the total weight must be listed
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. This connects to Chapter 11's red flags about plant labels. In a seed mix the composition figures are the product — without them you have no idea what proportion of what you are buying, or how much of it will come up.

    Concept Tested: State Seed Law

    See: Chapter 14

    9. Purple Loosestrife and Oriental Bittersweet are both regulated in Minnesota, but differently. What is the distinction?

    1. They are treated identically
    2. Oriental Bittersweet is Prohibited Eradicate — it must be destroyed wherever found — while Purple Loosestrife is Prohibited Control, meaning it must be controlled to prevent spread
    3. Purple Loosestrife is Restricted, meaning it may not be sold but need not be controlled
    4. Neither is regulated; both are merely discouraged
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The Noxious Weed Law's four tiers carry different legal obligations, and the tier reflects how far established a species already is. Eradicate is used where there is still a chance of removing the species from the state entirely.

    Concept Tested: Noxious Weed Categories

    See: Chapter 14

    10. You want to see mature examples of Minnesota native plant communities before designing your own garden. What does this chapter offer?

    1. Only photographs in books
    2. Public gardens and interpretive sites — Eloise Butler Wildflower Garden, the Minnesota Landscape Arboretum, Como Park Conservatory, county park programs and nature centers
    3. Only private gardens by appointment
    4. The University of Minnesota Bee Lab
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Seeing an established planting answers questions a plant list cannot — spacing, mature scale, and what a community looks like in each season. Eloise Butler in particular shows woodland ephemerals in May and Showy Lady's Slipper in June.

    Concept Tested: Public Gardens and Educational Sites

    See: Chapter 14
