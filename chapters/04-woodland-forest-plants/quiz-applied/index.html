    Applied Quiz: Woodland and Forest Plants

    A second set of questions on this chapter, weighted toward using the ideas rather than recalling them. Each one puts you in a situation and asks what the chapter's framework says to do.

    1. You visit a woodland in mid-July and see almost no wildflowers on the forest floor. A friend concludes the woods are degraded. What does this chapter suggest instead?

    1. She is right — a healthy woodland flowers all summer at ground level
    2. Spring ephemerals finish their whole above-ground season in four to six weeks and are dormant by June, so July tells you almost nothing; you have to visit in April or May to judge the ground layer
    3. The wildflowers were probably picked
    4. Deep shade means no plants can grow there at all
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter says it plainly: visit the same forest in July and you would never know they were there. Judging a woodland's ground layer in midsummer is a timing error, not an observation about quality.

    Concept Tested: Spring Ephemeral Life Cycle

    See: Chapter 4

    2. A developer says a woodland lot can be cleared and "replanted with the same wildflowers afterward." Using this chapter, what is wrong with that plan?

    1. Nothing — wildflowers transplant easily
    2. Spring ephemerals take years to establish and do not colonize disturbed sites quickly; a diverse ephemeral community is evidence of decades or centuries of stability, which cannot be replanted on a construction schedule
    3. It is fine as long as the same species are used
    4. Wildflowers would grow back on their own within one season
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter identifies spring ephemerals as indicators of high-quality, undisturbed forest for exactly this reason. Large-flowered Trillium alone can take seven to ten years from seed to first flower. The community is a record of time, and time is the part you cannot buy.

    Concept Tested: Ephemerals as Old-Forest Indicators

    See: Chapter 4

    3. Your yard has a mature oak canopy. You want to plant beneath it. Which of these choices fits the chapter's shade-tolerance spectrum?

    1. Bur Oak seedlings, since oaks belong under oaks
    2. Wild Ginger and Maidenhair Fern, which the chapter places at the very shade-tolerant end
    3. Purple Coneflower and Little Bluestem, for color
    4. Paper Birch, because it is a Minnesota native
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Option A is the trap worth noticing: Bur Oak is listed as shade intolerant, so oak seedlings do poorly under a closed oak canopy — which is a large part of why oak forests do not regenerate themselves without fire or gaps. Option C names prairie plants, and option D another shade-intolerant tree.

    Concept Tested: Shade Tolerance

    See: Chapter 4

    4. A Minnesota oak woods has had fire suppressed for a century. What does this chapter predict about its future composition?

    1. It stays an oak woods indefinitely
    2. Shade-tolerant maples gradually replace the shade-intolerant oaks, changing the character of the forest and the wildlife it supports
    3. It converts to prairie
    4. Oaks grow larger without fire, so the forest improves
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter names this succession directly. Oak seedlings need light that a closed canopy denies them, while sugar maple seedlings can wait in deep shade for decades until a gap opens. Fire used to reset that competition; without it, the outcome tilts one way.

    Concept Tested: Forest Succession

    See: Chapter 4

    5. You are trying to identify an unfamiliar woodland shrub about 8 feet tall. How does the forest-layer framework help before you even look at the leaves?

    1. It doesn't — layers are only useful for describing forests, not identifying plants
    2. Knowing it sits in the shrub layer tells you it grows in partial shade at 3–15 feet, which immediately narrows the candidate list and tells you a lot about its light and moisture needs
    3. It tells you the plant's exact species
    4. It tells you whether the plant is native
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter calls layer membership one of the most useful frameworks for learning woodland plants, precisely because layer implies conditions. It narrows the field; it does not name the species, which is why option C overstates it.

    Concept Tested: Forest Layers

    See: Chapter 4

    6. Why do spring ephemerals bloom before the trees leaf out, rather than waiting for warmer weather?

    1. They are poorly adapted and bloom too early by mistake
    2. Once the canopy closes it blocks 80–95% of sunlight; the weeks between snowmelt and leaf-out are the only period when enough light reaches the forest floor to fund a whole year's growth
    3. They need frost to trigger flowering
    4. Early blooming avoids competition from other ephemerals
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The strategy is about the light budget, not temperature. The plant does a year's photosynthesis in four to six weeks, stores it underground, and spends the rest of the year dormant. Blooming early is not a risk they tolerate — it is the entire point.

    Concept Tested: Canopy Light and Ephemeral Strategy

    See: Chapter 4

    7. Someone asks why a shade-tolerant seedling grows so slowly compared with one in an open field. What is the chapter's explanation?

    1. Shade-tolerant plants are genetically weaker
    2. Slow growth is part of the adaptation — a low metabolic rate lets the plant hold a positive energy balance on very little light, so it can wait years or decades for a canopy gap
    3. The soil under forests lacks nutrients
    4. Shade-tolerant plants grow fast but are eaten by deer
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter lists slow growth alongside large thin leaves, dark green color and efficient energy use as adaptations, not deficits. In deep shade, patience beats speed, and the payoff comes when a canopy tree falls.

    Concept Tested: Shade-Tolerance Adaptations

    See: Chapter 4

    8. You find a woodland with a dense buckthorn shrub layer and bare ground beneath it. Combining this chapter with Chapter 1, what has most likely happened to the ground layer?

    1. Deer ate everything and buckthorn is unrelated
    2. Buckthorn has taken over the shrub layer and leafs out early and holds late, so the light window the spring ephemerals depend on has been closed
    3. The soil has become too fertile
    4. Nothing — bare ground is normal beneath a shrub layer
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. This is the two chapters working together. The ephemeral strategy depends entirely on the gap between snowmelt and canopy leaf-out, and buckthorn's extended leaf season closes exactly that gap. Deer pressure is a real compounding factor, which is why option A is wrong only in ruling buckthorn out.

    Concept Tested: Invasive Shrubs and the Ground Layer

    See: Chapter 4

    9. A gardener plants Virginia Bluebells and Wild Ginger together in the same shady bed. By August the bluebells have vanished and she thinks they died. What actually happened?

    1. Wild Ginger outcompeted and killed them
    2. Virginia Bluebells are a spring ephemeral and go dormant underground by midsummer; Wild Ginger holds its leaves all season, so the pairing works but leaves a gap the gardener should plan for
    3. They were planted too deep
    4. Bluebells are annuals and need replanting each year
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. This is a practical consequence of the ephemeral life cycle: pairing ephemerals with season-long companions like ferns or Wild Ginger is a design technique, because something has to occupy the space after the ephemerals retreat.

    Concept Tested: Designing with Spring Ephemerals

    See: Chapter 4

    10. A homeowner wants a small native tree for a shaded spot beneath a large canopy. Which reasoning matches this chapter?

    1. Any Minnesota native tree will do, since they all grow in Minnesota
    2. Choose an understory species adapted to filtered light — Ironwood or Pagoda Dogwood, which the chapter places in the 15–40 ft understory layer
    3. Choose White Pine, because it grows tallest
    4. Choose Bur Oak, because oaks support the most caterpillar species
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Ironwood and Pagoda Dogwood are the chapter's own examples of the understory layer, which is defined by filtered light. Option D cites a true and important fact from elsewhere in the book, but Bur Oak is shade-intolerant and would struggle under a canopy — a good reason applied to the wrong site.
